# 2000–1000 BCE: Bronze Age Kingdoms

The second millennium BCE was a golden age of interconnected palace economies spanning the entire Old World from the Aegean to the Yellow River. Great powers negotiated on near-equal terms: Egyptians sent gold, Babylonians sent lapis lazuli, Hittites sent iron, and Mycenaeans sent tin-bronze weapons and wine. Diplomatic correspondence discovered at Tell el-Amarna (Egypt) preserves letters between pharaohs and the kings of Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni, and Hatti — addressed to each other as "brothers" and exchanging gifts and royal daughters.

This cosmopolitan Bronze Age order ended catastrophically around 1200 BCE in the mysterious Bronze Age Collapse, one of the most dramatic civilizational failures in history, involving the destruction of virtually every major palace center in the eastern Mediterranean.

## Key Events

- **c. 2000 BCE**: Rise of Babylon as a major Mesopotamian city
- **c. 1792 BCE**: Hammurabi becomes king of Babylon; issues his famous law code, one of the earliest comprehensive legal systems
- **c. 1700 BCE**: The Hyksos, a West Semitic people, invade and rule Lower Egypt (Second Intermediate Period)
- **c. 1600 BCE**: Rise of the Hittite Old Kingdom in Anatolia; Mycenaean civilization emerges in Greece
- **c. 1550 BCE**: Ahmose I expels the Hyksos and founds Egypt's New Kingdom
- **c. 1450 BCE**: The Shang Dynasty in China is at its height; oracle bone inscriptions are the earliest Chinese writing
- **c. 1350 BCE**: Akhenaten introduces monotheistic worship of the Aten in Egypt — a short-lived religious revolution
- **c. 1274 BCE**: Battle of Kadesh between Ramesses II and the Hittites — the first battle recorded in detail in history
- **c. 1200 BCE**: The Bronze Age Collapse; widespread destruction of palace centers from Greece to Syria, end of the Hittite Empire, end of Mycenaean civilization
- **c. 1046 BCE**: The Zhou Dynasty overthrows the Shang in China

## Major Civilizations

### Babylonian Mesopotamia
Hammurabi (r. 1792–1750 BCE) transformed Babylon from one city-state among many into the dominant power in Mesopotamia. His law code, inscribed on a black basalt stele, covered commerce, marriage, property, and crime with nuanced case law — demonstrating an advanced legal culture. The code's famous "eye for an eye" principle was actually a moderating provision: it replaced unlimited vengeance with proportional justice.

### New Kingdom Egypt
Under the warrior-pharaohs of Dynasties 18 through 20, Egypt became an imperial power. Thutmose III conducted seventeen military campaigns into the Levant. Amenhotep III presided over a court of extraordinary luxury. Akhenaten's monotheistic revolution provoked a crisis that Tutankhamun reversed. Ramesses II (r. 1279–1213 BCE) reigned for 67 years, fathered over 100 children, and erected more monuments than any other pharaoh.

### The Bronze Age Collapse
Around 1200 BCE, the interconnected palace-based economies of the eastern Mediterranean collapsed in a span of perhaps fifty years. Ugarit, the great Levantine trading metropolis, was burned and never rebuilt. The Hittite capital Hattusa was abandoned. Mycenae, Tiryns, and dozens of Greek palace centers were destroyed. Egypt survived but shrunken. The cause remains debated: drought, famine, the migrations of the "Sea Peoples," internal rebellions, and earthquake are all invoked, most likely in combination.

## Legacy

The Bronze Age world demonstrated that geographically dispersed civilizations could sustain complex interdependence — and that this interdependence created systemic fragility. The collapse of 1200 BCE and the subsequent Dark Age produced, ultimately, the alphabetic writing, iron technology, and decentralized political forms that would underpin classical civilization.
