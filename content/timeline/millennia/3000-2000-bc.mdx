# 3000–2000 BCE: Pyramid Age and Early Empires

The third millennium BCE witnessed the first great flowering of monumental civilization. Egypt's Old Kingdom erected structures that would remain the tallest buildings on earth for nearly four thousand years. Mesopotamia saw the first multi-ethnic territorial empire under Sargon of Akkad. The Indus Valley cities of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa achieved levels of urban planning — grid streets, standardized brick sizes, sophisticated sewage systems — that would not be matched in Europe until the Roman period. Across Eurasia, Bronze Age trade networks carried metals, textiles, and ideas over thousands of miles.

This was an era defined by the concentration of wealth and power in the service of ideology: divine kingship expressed through monuments, temples, and warfare. The scale of collective human effort marshaled for construction projects like the Giza pyramids or the ziggurat of Ur remains staggering even by modern standards.

## Key Events

- **c. 2686 BCE**: Egypt's Old Kingdom begins; the age of pyramid building commences
- **c. 2560 BCE**: The Great Pyramid of Giza is completed under Pharaoh Khufu — 2.3 million stone blocks
- **c. 2500 BCE**: Mohenjo-daro and Harappa are at their peak; the Indus Civilization covers an area larger than ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia combined
- **c. 2334 BCE**: Sargon of Akkad conquers the Sumerian city-states and creates history's first empire, stretching from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean
- **c. 2200 BCE**: The Akkadian Empire collapses, possibly due to a prolonged drought — an early example of climate-driven civilizational stress
- **c. 2112 BCE**: The Third Dynasty of Ur (Ur III) rises in Mesopotamia, creating a highly bureaucratic state with elaborate record-keeping
- **c. 2000 BCE**: Bronze Age begins in China; Longshan culture in the Yellow River valley

## Major Civilizations

### Egypt's Old Kingdom
The Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BCE) represents the first great unified expression of Egyptian civilization. A succession of powerful pharaohs commanded the resources of the entire Nile Valley to build mortuary complexes of breathtaking scale. Djoser's Step Pyramid at Saqqara (c. 2650 BCE) was the first large stone structure ever built. Within a century, the art of pyramid construction reached its zenith at Giza. The theological logic was clear: the pharaoh was a living god; his eternal house must be proportionally eternal.

### The Akkadian Empire
Sargon of Akkad (r. c. 2334–2279 BCE) was history's first empire-builder. Beginning as a cup-bearer to the king of Kish, he overthrew his master, conquered the Sumerian city-states one by one, and extended his rule to the Mediterranean coast, Anatolia, and possibly the Indus trade routes. He introduced Akkadian as an administrative language alongside Sumerian, creating a genuinely multicultural imperial bureaucracy. His daughter, Enheduanna, composed hymns to the goddess Inanna — the earliest named author in human history.

### The Indus Valley Civilization
Between approximately 2600 and 1900 BCE, a remarkable urban civilization flourished across the floodplains of the Indus River and its tributaries. Its largest cities — Mohenjo-daro and Harappa — housed populations of perhaps 40,000–80,000 people. The cities were built on a standardized grid plan with covered drainage systems. Their script remains undeciphered, leaving us ignorant of their language, rulers, and religion. They traded with Mesopotamia and left behind exquisitely carved stone seals.

## Legacy

The third millennium established the templates for imperial statecraft, monumental propaganda, and long-distance commerce that would define the ancient world for the next two thousand years. The collapse of both the Akkadian Empire and the Indus Valley civilization also provided an early warning: civilizations are fragile, and climate is a decisive actor in history.
