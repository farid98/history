philosophy:
  - name: "Hegel and Marx"
    summary: "Hegel's dialectic of history as Geist realising itself; Marx inverted it into material class struggle"
    detail: "G.W.F. Hegel (1770–1831) argued that history is the self-realisation of Geist (Spirit or Mind) through a dialectical process of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis — contradictions are the engine of progress, and the Prussian constitutional state was (he controversially claimed) the culmination of this process. Karl Marx (1818–1883) took the dialectic and inverted it: the driving force of history is not Idea but material conditions — specifically, class conflict over the means of production. Capitalism would generate its own gravediggers (the proletariat), leading to revolution and a classless society. Few ideas have more directly shaped the 20th century."
  - name: "Existentialism"
    summary: "Kierkegaard's leap of faith, Nietzsche's death of God, Sartre's existence precedes essence — philosophy confronts radical freedom"
    detail: "Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) insisted that the religious life — a subjective leap of faith into the absurd — is higher than both the aesthetic and the ethical. Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) declared God dead (killed by the Enlightenment's own honesty) and challenged humanity to create new values rather than inheriting Christian morality. Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) concluded that if there is no God, there is no human nature either: 'existence precedes essence' — we are condemned to be free, and bad faith consists in pretending otherwise. Existentialism became the defining philosophical movement of the post-war world, expressed as much in literature and film as in academic treatises."
  - name: "Analytic Philosophy"
    summary: "Russell and Wittgenstein diagnosed philosophy's problems as linguistic; logical positivism demanded verification"
    detail: "Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) and G.E. Moore began the analytic tradition by attacking Hegelian idealism with logical rigour. Ludwig Wittgenstein's Tractatus (1921) argued that philosophy's pseudo-problems arise from misuse of language: 'Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.' The Vienna Circle developed logical positivism: a statement is meaningful only if it is analytically true or empirically verifiable. This criterion, intended to banish metaphysics, itself turned out to be neither analytically true nor empirically verifiable — but the demand for clarity and rigour it imposed transformed Anglophone philosophy permanently."
  - name: "Pragmatism"
    summary: "James and Dewey: truth is what works; philosophy should address real problems rather than unanswerable metaphysical questions"
    detail: "William James (1842–1910) and John Dewey (1859–1952) developed the distinctively American philosophical tradition of pragmatism. James argued that the truth of an idea consists in its practical consequences — 'the cash value' of a concept is what difference it makes to our experience. Dewey applied this to education, democracy, and social reform: philosophy should not dwell on eternal verities but on the tools humans need to solve real problems. Pragmatism's influence on American law (Oliver Wendell Holmes), education, and social science was immense, and it anticipated later post-analytic philosophy's suspicion of metaphysical certainty."

# 1800–2000 CE: The Modern Age

The two centuries from 1800 to 2000 CE transformed human existence more radically than any comparable period in history. The Industrial Revolution harnessed fossil fuels to replace human and animal muscle with machines, producing a sustained explosion in material wealth — and an equally sustained explosion in inequality, urbanization, and environmental impact. Two world wars killed over 80 million people and reshaped the political map of the globe. The Cold War between the United States and Soviet Union brought humanity to the brink of nuclear annihilation. And the digital revolution, beginning in the final decades of the century, inaugurated an information age whose long-term consequences remain incalculable.

The period also witnessed the near-universal abolition of formal slavery, the enfranchisement of women across most of the world, decolonization of European empires, the founding of the United Nations, and unprecedented advances in medicine, science, and human longevity.

## Key Events

- **1804 CE**: Napoleon crowns himself Emperor of France; Haitian Revolution succeeds — the only successful slave revolt in history
- **1815 CE**: Congress of Vienna establishes a new European order after Napoleon
- **1848 CE**: Year of Revolutions sweeps Europe; Marx and Engels publish *The Communist Manifesto*
- **1865 CE**: Abolition of slavery in the United States following the Civil War
- **1869 CE**: Suez Canal opens, dramatically shortening sea routes between Europe and Asia
- **1884–1885 CE**: Berlin Conference divides Africa among European powers
- **1905 CE**: Einstein publishes special relativity; revolution in Russia
- **1914–1918 CE**: World War I kills 20 million; empires of Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia, and the Ottomans collapse
- **1917 CE**: Bolshevik Revolution in Russia
- **1918–1919 CE**: Spanish Flu pandemic kills 50–100 million — the deadliest pandemic in modern history
- **1929 CE**: Wall Street Crash and the Great Depression
- **1933 CE**: Hitler takes power in Germany
- **1939–1945 CE**: World War II kills 70–85 million; Holocaust murders 6 million Jews; atomic bombs destroy Hiroshima and Nagasaki
- **1947–1948 CE**: Indian independence; founding of Israel; partition of Korea
- **1948 CE**: Universal Declaration of Human Rights adopted
- **1953 CE**: Watson and Crick describe the structure of DNA
- **1957 CE**: Sputnik — the Space Age begins
- **1969 CE**: Apollo 11 lands on the Moon
- **1989 CE**: Fall of the Berlin Wall; Tiananmen Square massacre in China
- **1991 CE**: Soviet Union dissolves; end of the Cold War
- **1991 CE**: World Wide Web invented by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN

## Major Developments

### The Industrial Revolution
Beginning in Britain around 1760–1800, the Industrial Revolution replaced animate power with inanimate power at an exponential rate. Steam engines, railways, and steel mills transformed Britain from an agricultural to an industrial society within two generations. The process spread to Western Europe, North America, and Japan in the 19th century. Real wages roughly doubled in Britain between 1800 and 1870, while life expectancy, literacy, and urbanization rose dramatically.

### The Age of Imperialism
By 1900, European powers directly controlled 84% of the Earth's land surface. The British Empire alone spanned one quarter of the globe. Colonial rule extracted resources, disrupted traditional economies, redrawn political boundaries, and imposed European languages and religions — transformations whose consequences the world still lives with. Resistance to colonialism fed into the nationalist movements that produced decolonization in the 1940s–1960s.

### The World Wars
The First World War, triggered by the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914, consumed the flower of a generation in industrial-scale trench warfare. Its resolution planted the seeds of the Second: the humiliating Treaty of Versailles, the Great Depression, and the rise of fascism. The Second World War was incomparably more destructive, culminating in the Holocaust and the first use of nuclear weapons. The postwar settlement created the United Nations, the International Monetary Fund, NATO, and the global order that persisted to the end of the century.

### The Cold War and Nuclear Age
From 1947 to 1991, the United States and Soviet Union divided the world into competing spheres of influence, fought dozens of proxy wars, and accumulated enough nuclear weapons to destroy civilization many times over. The Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 brought the world within hours of nuclear war. The Cold War also drove the Space Race, which produced satellites, GPS, and eventually the internet.

## Legacy

The 20th century simultaneously produced the worst violence in human history and the greatest improvements in human welfare. Extreme poverty, infant mortality, illiteracy, and disease all declined dramatically in absolute terms while GDP per capita multiplied many times. The century also bequeathed to the future the threats of climate change, nuclear proliferation, and the existential risks of emerging technologies.
