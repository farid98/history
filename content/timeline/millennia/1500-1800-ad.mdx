# 1500–1800 CE: The Early Modern World

The three centuries between 1500 and 1800 CE constitute what historians call the "Early Modern" period — the hinge between the medieval world and modernity. European maritime expansion brought all the world's civilizations into contact, collision, and exchange for the first time. The consequences were transformative and deeply asymmetric: the indigenous civilizations of the Americas were devastated by disease and conquest; African populations were forcibly transported to the New World in the largest forced migration in history; while Europe itself was convulsed by religious warfare, intellectual revolution, and the slow emergence of capitalism.

The Scientific Revolution fundamentally altered humanity's relationship with the natural world, replacing Aristotelian cosmology with the heliocentric universe of Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton. The Enlightenment applied similar critical methods to politics and society, producing the concepts of natural rights, constitutionalism, and human progress that would animate the revolutions to come.

## Key Events

- **1492 CE**: Columbus reaches the Caribbean, beginning the Columbian Exchange
- **1494 CE**: Treaty of Tordesillas divides the non-European world between Spain and Portugal
- **1517 CE**: Martin Luther posts his 95 Theses, igniting the Protestant Reformation
- **1519–1521 CE**: Hernan Cortes conquers the Aztec Empire
- **1519–1522 CE**: Magellan's fleet completes the first circumnavigation of the globe
- **1526 CE**: Babur founds the Mughal Empire in India
- **1543 CE**: Copernicus publishes *On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres*
- **1545–1563 CE**: Council of Trent — the Catholic Counter-Reformation
- **1571 CE**: Battle of Lepanto; the Ottoman advance into the western Mediterranean is halted
- **1600 CE**: British East India Company founded; Dutch East India Company follows in 1602
- **1618–1648 CE**: Thirty Years' War devastates Central Europe; Peace of Westphalia establishes sovereignty as the basis of international order
- **1687 CE**: Newton publishes *Principia Mathematica*, the foundation of classical physics
- **1689 CE**: English Bill of Rights — a landmark in constitutional monarchy
- **1700–1800 CE**: The peak of the transatlantic slave trade; approximately 6 million enslaved Africans transported
- **1762–1796 CE**: Catherine the Great rules Russia, expanding its territory and patronizing Enlightenment thought
- **1776 CE**: American Declaration of Independence, drawing explicitly on Enlightenment principles
- **1789 CE**: French Revolution begins; the Declaration of the Rights of Man is proclaimed

## Major Developments

### The Columbian Exchange
The biological and cultural exchange between the Old and New Worlds after 1492 was one of the most dramatic events in ecological history. European diseases — smallpox, measles, influenza — to which American populations had no immunity killed an estimated 50–90% of the indigenous population of the Americas within a century. In the other direction, American crops — maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco — transformed diets and populations across Europe, Africa, and Asia. The potato alone is credited with supporting the population growth of early modern Europe.

### The Reformation and Its Wars
Luther's challenge to papal authority unleashed a century and a half of religious war. The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) killed perhaps eight million people — roughly a quarter of Central Europe's population. Its resolution at Westphalia established the principle of state sovereignty and religious toleration that became the basis of the modern international order.

### The Scientific Revolution
In a little over a century, European natural philosophers dismantled the Aristotelian-Ptolemaic worldview that had prevailed for two thousand years. Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, Descartes, and Newton replaced geocentrism with heliocentrism, impetus theory with the laws of motion, and occult sympathies with mathematical physics. The method they developed — systematic observation, mathematical modeling, experimental testing — proved universally applicable.

### The Transatlantic Slave Trade
Between 1500 and 1900, approximately 12.5 million Africans were captured and transported to the Americas. Perhaps 2 million died during the Middle Passage. This forced labor underpinned the plantation economies of the Caribbean and the American South, generating the capital that partly funded the Industrial Revolution. Its demographic, psychological, and political consequences shaped the Atlantic world for centuries.

## Legacy

The Early Modern period created the global economic system, established the nation-state as the primary unit of political organization, initiated the Scientific Revolution that continues to accelerate, and encoded the racial hierarchies of slavery and colonialism that humanity is still grappling with. The Enlightenment's promise of universal human rights and the actuality of slave plantations stood in stark, generative contradiction.
