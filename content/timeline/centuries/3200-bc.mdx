# 3200 BCE: Hieroglyphics and Proto-Writing

Around 3200 BCE, hieroglyphic writing appeared along the Nile Valley — apparently independently of cuneiform. The earliest Egyptian inscriptions, found in tombs at Abydos (the royal necropolis of the late Predynastic and early Dynastic kings), record names and possibly commodity labels in a system of pictographic signs. Unlike cuneiform, which began as pure accounting notation and only gradually developed phonetic elements, Egyptian hieroglyphics seem to have been designed from early on to record royal names and historical commemorations.

This parallel invention of writing in Egypt and Mesopotamia within a few centuries is one of the most striking facts of ancient history. Some scholars argue that the idea of writing diffused from Mesopotamia to Egypt even if the specific signs were independently invented. Others maintain full independent invention. Either way, the two writing systems developed very differently, serving different social purposes in different political contexts.

## Key Developments

- **c. 3200 BCE**: Earliest Egyptian hieroglyphic inscriptions at Abydos — tomb labels and royal name signs
- **c. 3200 BCE**: Newgrange passage tomb completed in Ireland — designed to capture the winter solstice sunrise
- **c. 3200 BCE**: The Indus Valley sees early settlements that will grow into the Harappan civilization
- **c. 3200 BCE**: Uruk at or near its population peak; the classic Uruk period ending
- **Proto-Elamite writing**: The Proto-Elamite script, still undeciphered, is in use across a wide area of Iran
- **Jemdet Nasr period**: A brief transition period in Mesopotamia between the Uruk and Early Dynastic periods; colorful polychrome pottery characterizes it

## Newgrange
The Newgrange passage tomb in the Boyne Valley, Ireland, was completed around 3200 BCE. Its carefully engineered roofbox is aligned so precisely that for five days around the winter solstice, a narrow beam of sunrise light travels 19 meters down the passage to illuminate the inner chamber for about 17 minutes. The structure predates Stonehenge and the Egyptian pyramids. Its builders had no writing, no metal tools, and no wheeled vehicles — and yet they produced a monument of astonishing astronomical precision.
