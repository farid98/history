# 3400 BCE: World's First Metropolis

By approximately 3400 BCE, the city of Uruk had grown to somewhere between 40,000 and 80,000 inhabitants, surrounded by a wall roughly ten kilometers in circumference. It was, by any definition, a city — and the world's first. Nothing remotely comparable existed anywhere else on earth. Its population exceeded any single community that had ever existed in the human species' 200,000-year history.

What made Uruk urban was not just its size but its complexity. It had a dense monumental center around the Eanna temple precinct, with residential quarters spreading outward. It had full-time specialists — priests, scribes, smiths, potters, weavers — who produced goods and services rather than growing food. It had an administrative apparatus managing the flows of labor, commodities, and tribute that sustained this complexity. And it was embedded in a regional network of smaller towns and villages that supplied raw materials and labor.

## Key Developments

- **Uruk's metropolitan scale**: The city's 6 km² built area and massive temple precinct represent an urban density and monumental investment without precedent
- **Cylinder seals**: These small carved stone cylinders could be rolled across wet clay to produce a continuous impression — used to seal documents and containers, they are the ancient equivalent of a personal signature
- **Numerical notation**: Complex numerical systems in the Uruk tablets allow accounting at city scale
- **Egyptian sail**: The earliest depictions of sail-powered boats appear in Egyptian Naqada II art — enabling navigation against the Nile's current and revolutionizing trade
- **Arsenical copper**: Bronze-like arsenical copper (harder than pure copper) begins to supplement stone tools in the Near East

## Life in Uruk
Archaeological evidence suggests a city organized around the redistribution of agricultural surpluses. Workers — weavers, brewers, potters, laborers — received daily rations of grain (about 1.5 liters) and beer (about 1 liter) recorded on tablets. The temple functioned simultaneously as granary, workshop, treasury, and divine house. The earliest economic documents record male and female workers, suggesting a mixed-gender labor force managed by the temple administration.
