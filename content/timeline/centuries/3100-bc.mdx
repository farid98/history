# 3100 BCE: Unification of Egypt

Around 3100 BCE, the ruler Narmer (sometimes identified with the legendary Menes) unified Upper and Lower Egypt under a single crown, founding the First Dynasty. This was the birth of the world's first territorial state — an enduring political entity governing a defined territory with a single supreme ruler, a professional administrative class, a standing military, and an official religion.

The Narmer Palette, a ceremonial cosmetic palette found at Hierakonpolis and now in the Cairo Museum, commemorates this unification. On one side, Narmer wears the White Crown of Upper Egypt and smites an enemy; on the other, he wears the Red Crown of Lower Egypt and reviews rows of decapitated enemies. The image is already using the canonical conventions of Egyptian royal art that would persist for three thousand years: the king shown at heroic scale, the enemy beneath his feet, the ordered rows of subjects.

## Key Developments

- **c. 3100 BCE**: Narmer/Menes unifies Egypt; the First Dynasty begins
- **c. 3100 BCE**: Memphis is founded as Egypt's capital at the junction of Upper and Lower Egypt
- **c. 3100 BCE**: The Narmer Palette — the first historical narrative in visual art
- **c. 3100 BCE**: Egyptian hieroglyphic writing is now clearly functional as an administrative script
- **c. 3100 BCE**: Bronze (tin-copper alloy) begins to appear in the Near East; the Bronze Age begins
- **c. 3000 BCE**: The Early Dynastic period begins in Mesopotamia; city-states like Ur, Kish, and Lagash are distinct polities
- **c. 3000 BCE**: The Indus Valley sees the beginning of what will become the Harappan civilization

## The Birth of the State
Egypt in 3100 BCE was unique: the world's first territorial state with continuous borders. Mesopotamia was organized as city-states — urban centers each governing their surrounding territory, but without a unified political authority. Egypt, by contrast, was a single ruler governing a ribbon of territory 1,200 km long. This was possible because the Nile itself created a natural communication and supply highway, making unified governance feasible in a way it was not in the more complex terrain of Mesopotamia.

The pharaonic institution — the god-king who mediated between the human and divine worlds — would endure for three thousand years, making it perhaps the most long-lived political institution in human history.
