# 3600 BCE: Uruk Expansion

The century around 3600 BCE marks an intensification of the "Uruk expansion" — the spread of southern Mesopotamian material culture, administrative practices, and possibly population across much of the Near East. Archaeological sites from Turkey (Arslantepe, Hacınebi) to Syria (Habuba Kabira) to Iran (Susa) show the sudden appearance of Uruk-style pottery, cylinder seals, numerical tablets, and architectural forms.

Scholars debate whether this expansion represents formal colonies established to control trade routes, mercantile diasporas of Uruk merchants, elite emulation by local rulers, or some combination. What is clear is that Uruk was not just growing internally but was projecting its influence — and perhaps its administrative technology — far beyond the Sumerian heartland.

## Key Developments

- **Uruk expansion**: Uruk-style materials appear at sites across the Near East — evidence of trade, colonies, or cultural diffusion
- **Habuba Kabira**: A planned Uruk-style town on the Euphrates in Syria — possibly a trading colony with grid streets and Uruk administrative tools
- **Sail-powered boats**: The earliest known depictions of sailing vessels date to approximately 3500 BCE in Egypt — the sail transforms long-distance trade
- **Varna goldwork**: The Varna Chalcolithic cemetery in Bulgaria (c. 4600–4200 BCE, slightly before this century) had already produced the oldest gold jewelry in the world; this tradition continues
- **Proto-cuneiform development**: At Uruk, the pictographic accounting system continues to develop toward a true script

## Why Did Uruk Expand?
The most likely explanation for the Uruk expansion is resource hunger. The Mesopotamian alluvial plain is extraordinarily fertile but almost completely lacks timber, stone, and metal ores. Uruk needed these materials for construction, tools, and prestige goods. The expansion likely represents a systematic effort to secure access to raw materials through colonies, trading posts, and relationships with local elites in resource-rich regions.
