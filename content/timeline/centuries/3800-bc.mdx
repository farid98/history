# 3800 BCE: The Uruk Period Begins

Around 3800 BCE, the city of Uruk in southern Mesopotamia entered the extraordinary period of growth that would make it the world's first true metropolis. The Uruk period (c. 4000–3100 BCE) takes its name from this city, and it was here that the convergence of population density, agricultural surplus, religious authority, and administrative need would produce writing, monumental architecture, and the first urban civilization.

The temple complex at Uruk, centered on the Eanna precinct, grew to encompass multiple temples, storehouses, and workshops. The Sumerian concept of the temple as the house of the city's patron deity — and therefore as the economic center managing the redistribution of agricultural surpluses — was taking its classic form. Priests and scribes were beginning to develop the clay token accounting systems that would evolve into cuneiform writing.

## Key Developments

- **Uruk's population growth**: The city grows from a large village to a town of several thousand, on its way to becoming a metropolis of 40,000–50,000 by 3400 BCE
- **Clay tokens**: Shaped clay tokens enclosed in clay envelopes (bullae) record commodity transactions — the direct precursor of cuneiform writing
- **Eanna temple precinct**: Construction of multi-room temple complexes at Uruk begins
- **Naqada II expansion**: Egypt's Naqada II culture (c. 3500–3200 BCE) begins; it will spread northward along the Nile, laying the groundwork for political unification
- **Specialized craft production**: Pottery wheels, kilns, and specialization of craft workers appear in proto-urban centers

## Accounting Before Writing
The earliest proto-writing at Uruk was not literature but accountancy. Clay tokens of various shapes (spheres, cones, disks) represented different commodities — a sphere for grain, a cone for a small measure, a cylinder for oil. These tokens were enclosed in clay envelopes marked with impressions of the tokens inside. Eventually, scribes realized they could simply impress the token shapes directly onto flat clay tablets — eliminating the need for the tokens themselves. Writing grew from this accounting tradition.
