# 3500 BCE: Writing and the Wheel

The century around 3500 BCE produced two of the most consequential inventions in human history: writing and the wheeled vehicle. Both emerged from the pressing practical needs of an increasingly complex agricultural society; both transformed that society in ways that their inventors could not have anticipated.

The earliest cuneiform signs at Uruk were accountancy: pictographs for grain, beer, oil, animals, and numbers, impressed into clay tablets by temple scribes managing the redistribution of commodities. Within centuries these signs would evolve into a full phonetic script capable of recording any utterance — and the full complexity of human thought became preservable beyond the span of a single life.

The wheeled vehicle appeared in the Mesopotamian alluvial plain and the Pontic steppe almost simultaneously, perhaps through independent invention, perhaps through rapid diffusion along trade routes. The ox-drawn cart dramatically reduced the energy cost of moving agricultural produce and building materials.

## Key Developments

- **c. 3400 BCE**: The Uruk Tablet (now in the British Museum) — among the very earliest cuneiform pictographs; records grain distributions
- **c. 3500 BCE**: The earliest wheeled vehicles; the potter's wheel comes into widespread use
- **c. 3500 BCE**: The plow pulled by draft animals is in use in Mesopotamia — increasing the area a farmer can cultivate
- **c. 3500 BCE**: The city of Uruk reaches a population of perhaps 20,000–25,000 people
- **c. 3500 BCE**: Bronze (tin-copper alloy) begins to appear, though pure copper tools remain more common
- **Naqada II culture in Egypt**: Artistic and cultural forms that will characterize pharaonic Egypt begin to crystallize

## The Logistics of Urban Life
A city of 20,000 people requires roughly 6,000 tonnes of grain per year — far more than could be stored in any single household. The temple of Uruk managed this redistribution: workers received rations of grain and beer in return for labor, recorded on clay tablets. The administrative apparatus this required — scribal schools, standardized measures, clay seals — was the direct ancestor of modern bureaucracy.
