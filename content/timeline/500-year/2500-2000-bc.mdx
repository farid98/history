# 2500–2000 BCE: Akkadian Empire and the First Collapse

The period from 2500 to 2000 BCE witnessed humanity's first experiment with imperial unification — and its first dramatic collapse. Sargon of Akkad's empire demonstrated that a single political authority could govern diverse peoples across a vast territory using professional armies, standardized administration, and a common written language. But it also demonstrated the fragility of such structures: within a century of its greatest extent, the Akkadian Empire had disintegrated.

The probable culprit was climate. Around 2200 BCE, a prolonged drought — the "4.2-kiloyear event" — struck the Old World from the Sahara to the Chinese Loess Plateau. In Mesopotamia, it caused crop failures, refugee movements, and the political fragmentation that ended the Akkadian Empire. In Egypt, it ended the Old Kingdom and produced a period of famine and regional rule. In the Indus Valley, it eventually contributed to the abandonment of the great cities.

## Key Developments

- **c. 2334 BCE**: Sargon of Akkad seizes power in Kish and begins his conquests
- **c. 2334–2279 BCE**: Sargon's campaigns unify Sumer and Akkad; the first territorial empire
- **c. 2285 BCE**: Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon, composes hymns to Inanna — the world's first named author
- **c. 2230 BCE**: Naram-Sin, Sargon's grandson, extends the empire to its maximum extent and declares himself a god
- **c. 2200 BCE**: The 4.2-kiloyear drought begins; crop failures hit Mesopotamia and Egypt
- **c. 2193 BCE**: The Akkadian Empire collapses under pressure from the Gutian invaders and internal revolts
- **c. 2181 BCE**: The Egyptian Old Kingdom ends; the First Intermediate Period of regional fragmentation begins
- **c. 2112 BCE**: The Third Dynasty of Ur (Ur III) restores centralized rule in Mesopotamia; the most bureaucratic state yet seen in human history
- **c. 2000 BCE**: Ur III collapses; the Indus Valley cities begin their long decline

## Enheduanna — The World's First Named Author
Enheduanna (c. 2285–2250 BCE) was the high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur and the daughter of Sargon. She composed 42 hymns to Sumerian temples and three major hymns to the goddess Inanna. That we know her name and her works survive makes her the earliest individual author in literary history. Her *Hymn to Inanna* is a passionate, first-person composition that reads remarkably like personal poetry despite its religious function.

## The Ur III Bureaucracy
The Third Dynasty of Ur created perhaps the most thoroughly documented administrative system of the ancient world. Hundreds of thousands of clay tablets record the movements of workers, allocations of rations, deliveries of goods, and temple accounts in minute detail. The Ur III state managed massive labor forces through a system of centralized assignment, creating something that resembles a planned economy more than a market.
