# 1–500 CE: The Roman Empire and Early Christianity

The first five centuries of the Common Era opened with the Mediterranean world unified under Roman rule and ended with the Western Roman Empire dissolved into Germanic successor kingdoms. In between lay the long Pax Romana (27 BCE – 180 CE), during which the Mediterranean basin enjoyed unprecedented peace, commerce, and urbanization; then the crisis of the third century; the Christianization of the empire; and finally the cascading pressures of climate change, pandemic, economic strain, and barbarian migration that ended Roman rule in the West.

This was also the period in which Christianity transformed from a small Jewish sect in Roman Palestine into the official religion of the empire — a transformation so complete that the very calendar of the Western world is dated from the approximate birth year of Jesus of Nazareth.

## Key Developments

- **c. 30 CE**: Crucifixion of Jesus of Nazareth
- **70 CE**: Roman general Titus destroys the Jerusalem Temple; end of the Second Temple period; accelerates the Jewish Diaspora
- **79 CE**: Vesuvius erupts; Pompeii and Herculaneum buried — preserved for archaeologists
- **98–117 CE**: Trajan expands the Roman Empire to its maximum extent
- **c. 100 CE**: Cai Lun refines papermaking in China; Tacitus writes the *Annals* and *Germania*
- **165–180 CE**: Antonine Plague kills 5–10 million in the Roman Empire
- **235–284 CE**: The Crisis of the Third Century — 50 emperors in 50 years; the empire nearly collapses
- **313 CE**: Edict of Milan legalizes Christianity
- **325 CE**: Council of Nicaea defines Christian orthodoxy
- **380 CE**: Theodosius declares Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire
- **410 CE**: Visigoths sack Rome
- **420 CE**: Fall of the Western Jin Dynasty in China; Period of Sixteen Kingdoms
- **476 CE**: Romulus Augustulus, last Western Roman Emperor, deposed by Odoacer
- **c. 500 CE**: Gupta Empire in India at its height — a golden age of Sanskrit literature, mathematics, and science

## The Pax Romana
The Roman peace (27 BCE – 180 CE) was not total tranquility, but within the empire's borders — a territory of some 50 million people — it created conditions for exceptional prosperity. Roman roads totaled 400,000 km. Roman aqueducts brought clean water to cities of hundreds of thousands. Roman law standardized property rights and contracts across the empire. The scale of urbanization would not be matched in Europe for a thousand years after Rome's fall.

## The Rise of Christianity
Jesus was executed as a threat to Roman order. His followers, convinced of his resurrection, created a small sect that spread slowly through the urban networks of the Roman Empire. Paul of Tarsus transformed a Jewish apocalyptic movement into a Gentile universal religion. By the fourth century, Christianity had millions of adherents across the empire. Constantine's conversion (c. 312 CE) and subsequent patronage gave the church resources, prestige, and eventually, under Theodosius, state power.
