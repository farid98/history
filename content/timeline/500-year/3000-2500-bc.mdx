# 3000–2500 BCE: Old Kingdom and Early Dynasties

In the five centuries between 3000 and 2500 BCE, the civilizations of the ancient Near East matured rapidly. In Egypt, the Early Dynastic period gave way to the Old Kingdom — the first great era of pyramid building and centralized pharaonic power. In Mesopotamia, the Early Dynastic period saw city-states like Ur, Lagash, Kish, and Uruk compete for dominance, generating a rich textual and artistic record. Trade networks linking Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia, Anatolia, and the Indus Valley carried not only goods but technologies, ideas, and people.

The key technological change of this period was the widespread adoption of bronze — an alloy of copper and tin that is harder and more durable than either metal alone. Bronze weapons gave military advantage; bronze tools improved agricultural and construction productivity. The need for tin, which occurs only in limited locations, drove long-distance trade and geopolitical competition.

## Key Developments

- **c. 2686 BCE**: Egypt's Third Dynasty begins; the Old Kingdom era of pyramid building commences
- **c. 2650 BCE**: Imhotep designs Djoser's Step Pyramid at Saqqara — the world's first large stone structure
- **c. 2600 BCE**: The Royal Tombs of Ur are constructed; excavated by Leonard Woolley in the 1920s, they revealed extraordinary wealth including the famous "death pits" with sacrificed retainers
- **c. 2600 BCE**: The Gilgamesh stories begin to circulate in Mesopotamia — later compiled as the world's oldest epic literature
- **c. 2550 BCE**: Sneferu builds the Bent Pyramid and Red Pyramid at Dashur — the first true pyramids
- **c. 2560 BCE**: The Great Pyramid of Giza commissioned by Khufu — 2.3 million stone blocks, 147 meters tall
- **c. 2500 BCE**: Mohenjo-daro and Harappa reach urban maturity; the Indus Valley Civilization at its height

## Egyptian Pyramid Technology
The construction of the Great Pyramid remains one of history's remarkable engineering achievements. Modern research suggests a workforce of 20,000–30,000 skilled laborers — not slaves, as popular belief holds, but workers who were fed, housed, and given medical care. The logistical organization required to feed this workforce, quarry and transport 2.3 million stone blocks averaging 2.5 tonnes each, and coordinate construction over 20 years was itself a feat of administrative genius.

## Sumerian Literature
The Early Dynastic period in Mesopotamia produced the world's first literary texts. The Kesh Temple Hymn (c. 2600 BCE) and early versions of the Gilgamesh stories are the oldest surviving narrative literature. These texts were produced in scribal schools (edubba, "tablet houses") and served both religious and pedagogical functions.
