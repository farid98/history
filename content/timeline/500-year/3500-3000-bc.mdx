# 3500–3000 BCE: The Urban Revolution

The half-millennium from 3500 to 3000 BCE saw the simultaneous emergence of writing, cities, and states — the three pillars of civilization — in both Mesopotamia and Egypt. Historians call this the "Urban Revolution," and its consequences were irreversible. Once writing existed, human memory could extend beyond a single lifetime. Once cities existed, the density of human interaction produced innovations that would never have emerged in dispersed villages. Once states existed, large-scale collective action — in building, warfare, and commerce — became possible.

The Uruk Period (c. 4000–3100 BCE) in Mesopotamia represents the first fully urban society in world history. The city of Uruk grew to perhaps 40,000–50,000 inhabitants, with specialized craft quarters, a dominant temple complex, and a literate administrative class managing the redistribution of agricultural surpluses. Its influence radiated across the Near East through what archaeologists call the "Uruk expansion" — colonies and trading posts that spread Sumerian material culture as far as Anatolia and Iran.

## Key Developments

- **c. 3400 BCE**: The earliest cuneiform tablets are produced at Uruk — pictographic signs for commodities and numbers that would evolve into a full writing system
- **c. 3400 BCE**: The wheel is applied to transport (wheeled vehicles appear in Mesopotamia and the Caucasus nearly simultaneously)
- **c. 3400 BCE**: The first cylinder seals appear — small carved stone cylinders that could impress a unique mark in clay, the ancient equivalent of a signature or brand
- **c. 3200 BCE**: Egyptian hieroglyphic script emerges, apparently independently of cuneiform
- **c. 3150 BCE**: Upper and Lower Egypt are unified by the first pharaohs of Dynasty 0 and Dynasty 1
- **c. 3100 BCE**: The Narmer Palette commemorates the unification of Egypt — the first historical narrative in art
- **c. 3000 BCE**: The city of Memphis is founded as Egypt's capital

## The Invention of Writing
The earliest cuneiform signs at Uruk were not used for literature but for bookkeeping: rations of grain and beer for laborers, tallies of livestock, land records. Writing was invented to solve the bureaucratic problems of a redistributive economy that had outgrown the capacity of unaided human memory. Poetry, myth, and law followed centuries later.

## Egypt's Unification
While Mesopotamia was organized as a landscape of competing city-states, Egypt's geography — a narrow river valley flanked by desert — encouraged unification. The process was gradual: the Naqada II culture (c. 3500–3200 BCE) shows increasing uniformity of material culture along the Nile before the political unification under Narmer around 3100 BCE.
