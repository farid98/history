# 1500–1000 BCE: New Kingdom Egypt and the Bronze Age Collapse

The period from 1500 to 1000 BCE encompasses both the zenith of the Late Bronze Age international order and its catastrophic collapse. For most of this period, the great powers — Egypt, the Hittites, Mitanni, Assyria, Babylonia, and the Aegean kingdoms — interacted through a sophisticated system of diplomacy, commerce, and royal intermarriage. Their mutual correspondence, preserved in the Amarna Letters, reveals a cosmopolitan world of "great kings" who traded daughters and gold and resolved disputes through negotiation.

Then, around 1200 BCE, this world ended. Within fifty years, virtually every major palace center from Greece to Syria was destroyed or abandoned. The Hittite Empire vanished. Mycenaean civilization collapsed into centuries of illiteracy. Ugarit — the great Levantine trading city — was burned and never rebuilt. Egypt survived but greatly weakened. The Bronze Age Collapse remains one of the most dramatic and debated catastrophes in ancient history.

## Key Developments

- **c. 1550 BCE**: Ahmose I expels the Hyksos and founds Egypt's Eighteenth Dynasty (New Kingdom)
- **c. 1479 BCE**: Thutmose III begins his 17 military campaigns into the Levant — the greatest Egyptian conqueror
- **c. 1353 BCE**: Akhenaten becomes pharaoh and introduces monotheistic worship of the sun disk Aten
- **c. 1335 BCE**: Tutankhamun restores traditional polytheism
- **c. 1274 BCE**: Battle of Kadesh — Ramesses II versus the Hittites; the first battle recorded in detail; the resulting treaty is the world's oldest surviving peace agreement
- **c. 1250 BCE**: The Exodus tradition: Israelite monotheism takes shape, possibly connected to Akhenaten's period or Ramesses II's reign
- **c. 1200 BCE**: The Sea Peoples begin to attack Egypt and the eastern Mediterranean
- **c. 1200 BCE**: Hittite capital Hattusa is burned and abandoned
- **c. 1200 BCE**: Mycenae, Tiryns, and other Greek palace centers are destroyed
- **c. 1050 BCE**: The Zhou Dynasty overthrows the Shang Dynasty in China

## Akhenaten's Religious Revolution
Akhenaten (r. 1353–1336 BCE) attempted to replace Egypt's complex polytheism with the exclusive worship of the Aten, the physical disk of the sun. He built a new capital (Amarna), suppressed other cults, and erased the name of Amun from monuments. After his death, his successors reversed all his changes and attempted to erase his memory. The connection between Atenism and later monotheism (particularly Israelite religion) is debated but suggestive.

## The Sea Peoples
The Bronze Age Collapse was partly driven by the migrations of groups collectively labeled the "Sea Peoples" by Egyptian sources. Their identity remains disputed: they may have included Mycenaean Greeks, Anatolians, and others displaced by earlier collapses in a cascading crisis. Ramesses III repelled them at the Battle of the Delta (c. 1175 BCE), but the effort left Egypt exhausted.
