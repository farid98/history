# 2000–1500 BCE: Age of Hammurabi

The first five centuries of the second millennium BCE saw a recovery and reconstruction after the collapses of the 22nd century. In Mesopotamia, the Old Babylonian period produced its most celebrated monument: the law code of Hammurabi. In Egypt, the Middle Kingdom represented a cultural and economic renaissance. In the Aegean, the Minoan civilization on Crete was developing Europe's first palatial society. On the Eurasian steppe, the chariot was being developed — a military technology that would transform warfare across the Old World within a century.

This was also the era in which the great Indo-European migrations began in earnest. Pastoral peoples from the Pontic steppe carried Indo-European languages into Anatolia (Hittite), Greece (Proto-Greek), Iran (Proto-Iranian), and India (Vedic Sanskrit). These migrations — probably a combination of conquest, elite diffusion, and gradual demographic expansion — fundamentally changed the linguistic map of Eurasia.

## Key Developments

- **c. 2000 BCE**: Middle Kingdom of Egypt begins under Mentuhotep II
- **c. 1900 BCE**: The Assyrian trading colonies at Kanesh in Anatolia — the oldest surviving commercial contracts in history
- **c. 1894 BCE**: The First Dynasty of Babylon is established
- **c. 1792 BCE**: Hammurabi becomes king of Babylon
- **c. 1754 BCE**: Hammurabi promulgates his law code — 282 case laws carved on an 8-foot basalt stele
- **c. 1750 BCE**: Hammurabi unifies Mesopotamia under Babylonian rule
- **c. 1700 BCE**: The Hyksos — West Semitic chariot warriors — enter and eventually dominate Lower Egypt
- **c. 1700 BCE**: The chariot spreads rapidly from the steppe across the Near East
- **c. 1650 BCE**: Minoan Crete at its cultural height; palace at Knossos rebuilt magnificently
- **c. 1600 BCE**: Vedic Aryans begin settling in the Indus Valley and Gangetic plains

## Hammurabi's Code
The Code of Hammurabi is not the oldest law code (the Ur-Nammu code of c. 2100 BCE is older) but it is the most complete and best-preserved. Its 282 laws cover contracts, property, family, and criminal matters with striking sophistication. The famous *lex talionis* (eye for an eye) principle was actually a restraining measure: it limited punishment to proportional retaliation and replaced the unlimited vengeance of blood feud with measured justice.

## Minoan Crete
The Minoan civilization (c. 2700–1450 BCE) was Europe's first complex palatial society. The Palace of Knossos covered 20,000 square meters with running water, flushing toilets, elaborate frescoes, and a sophisticated administrative apparatus using the undeciphered Linear A script. Minoan trade networks reached Egypt and the Levant. Their art — fluid, nature-loving, featuring dolphins, bulls, and bare-breasted goddesses — is utterly unlike anything else in the ancient world.
